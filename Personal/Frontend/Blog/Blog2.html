I May Have Gone Too Far...

Too far that I don't know where to go back lmao.

AUTHOR // JANE DATE // 09 OCT 2026 TIME // LOCATION UNKNOWN

I genuinely thought I was fucked.

I do not mean that in the casual, mildly inconvenienced sense of the phrase. I mean that, at some point during my little adventure, I looked around at the enormous public place I had found myself in and genuinely considered the possibility that I might never find my way out.

Somewhere out there was my sister.

Somewhere out there was an exit.

And somewhere between those two very important facts was me, a person with absolutely no reliable idea of where either of them happened to be.

It is a peculiar feeling, realising that you have become lost in a place that was designed to accommodate people.

There are signs. There are corridors. There are entire sections dedicated to helping visitors navigate the establishment. There are maps, directions, landmarks, and presumably several individuals whose entire working day consists of knowing where everything is.

And then there is me.

Wandering around as though I have been dropped into a civilisation whose customs I have not studied.

I had one objective: find my sister.

Somehow, this became an expedition.

At first, I was not particularly worried.

Getting separated from someone in a crowded public place is hardly an unprecedented catastrophe. People get distracted. Someone walks a little faster than expected. One person stops to look at something while the other continues ahead.

It happens.

I assumed I would simply walk around for a bit, recognise a familiar landmark, and reunite with my sister before the situation became anything worth remembering.

This was my first mistake.

I had apparently underestimated the sheer scale of the place.

What I thought would be a straightforward search turned into a succession of corridors, walkways, intersections, entrances, and sections that all seemed to possess the same architectural personality.

Everything looked familiar enough to be useless.

I would turn a corner and think, surely I have seen this before. Then I would continue walking and discover that the place had somehow produced another corridor from the same general collection of walls and floors.

It was as though the building had been designed by somebody who believed that human beings should be punished for developing a sense of direction.

I kept walking.

Because what else was I supposed to do?

Stand in the middle of the walkway and announce that I had been defeated by the concept of indoor architecture?

I had a sister to find. Dignity could wait.

The problem with getting lost is that the situation does not necessarily look serious from the outside.

From another person's perspective, I was probably just a girl walking around a public establishment.

Nothing unusual. Nothing alarming. Just another person going somewhere.

Meanwhile, inside my head, a full emergency meeting had been called.

WHERE AM I?

WHERE IS MY SISTER?

WHY DOES THIS PLACE HAVE SO MANY CORRIDORS?

WHO APPROVED THIS FLOOR PLAN?

WAS THE ARCHITECT ATTEMPTING TO CREATE A BUILDING OR A PERMANENT PSYCHOLOGICAL TRIAL?

I had reached that delightful stage of confusion where every new direction seemed equally plausible and equally likely to make matters worse.

Go left? Perhaps.

Go right? Also perhaps.

Return the way I came? A wonderful suggestion, provided I could remember which way I had come.

I had entered the building as a functioning person with a relatively ordinary objective.

I was now conducting a geographical investigation with no map, no useful evidence, and a steadily declining confidence in my own judgement.

I did not need an adventure. I needed to find my sister.

Unfortunately, the universe had apparently confused those two requirements.

Time began to behave strangely.

I cannot tell you precisely how long I had been wandering at every point, but the longer the search continued, the less meaningful the passage of time became.

I would walk for what felt like several minutes, only to discover that I had covered an insignificant distance relative to the enormous place surrounding me.

Then I would walk some more.

And some more.

And, because apparently I had not yet received the message, even more.

Every passing minute made the original situation feel increasingly ridiculous.

How had I managed to turn something as simple as finding a family member into a journey of this magnitude?

I was not crossing a wilderness. I was not trekking through an unexplored mountain range. I was not navigating an ancient ruin full of traps and suspiciously convenient treasure chests.

I was in a public place.

A place where people presumably arrived, completed whatever they had come to do, and left again without having to reconsider the fundamental reliability of their legs.

And yet I continued to wander.

At some point, I began to feel genuinely overwhelmed.

It was not simply that I could not find my sister. It was the realisation that I did not have a clear understanding of where I was in relation to anything else.

I could see people going about their business, and I could hear the ordinary noise of a busy establishment, but none of it helped me orient myself.

The place was full of people who appeared to know where they were going.

I found this deeply offensive.

How dare they possess such confidence in their own whereabouts while I was fighting for my life against a corridor?

They walked with purpose. They turned corners without hesitation. They approached entrances as though they had been informed of their existence in advance.

I watched them with the quiet envy of a person who had misplaced the most basic feature of a successful public outing.

Direction.

I began to consider the possibility that I was genuinely, spectacularly screwed.

Not permanently, I hoped. I was still aware that I was in a place inhabited by other human beings, and I understood that there were ways to ask for directions or find assistance.

But emotionally?

Emotionally, I had already written the incident report.

SUBJECT: JANE.

STATUS: LOST.

LAST KNOWN OBJECTIVE: LOCATE SISTER.

CURRENT PROGRESS: WALKING AROUND IN CIRCLES, ALLEGEDLY.

PROBABILITY OF LOOKING RIDICULOUS: INCREASING.

The situation was made worse by the fact that I could not simply convince myself that I was making progress.

If you walk for a long time and arrive somewhere recognisable, at least you have evidence that your efforts have accomplished something.

But when every corridor looks like another corridor, every intersection resembles the last, and every turn introduces yet another unfamiliar section of the same enormous place, walking becomes an activity of questionable value.

I was expending energy without gaining certainty.

I was moving without knowing whether the movement was helping.

And, perhaps most humiliatingly, I had no one to blame for the entire situation except the building and my own increasingly questionable navigation strategy.

I would have blamed my sister, but she was the person I was trying to find. It seemed premature to file a complaint against the missing party.

Somewhere in this enormous establishment was my sister. Somewhere in this enormous establishment was also my ability to make sensible decisions.

Neither appeared willing to reveal its location.

The truly absurd part is that the objective never changed.

I did not suddenly decide to explore the establishment for recreational purposes. I did not develop a passionate interest in its architecture. I did not wake up that morning intending to conduct a comprehensive survey of its walkways.

I wanted to find my sister.

That was it.

One person. One objective. A reasonably straightforward problem that had somehow expanded to occupy the entirety of my afternoon.

I imagine there is something poetic about that.

I would prefer the poetry to leave me alone.

There is nothing particularly profound about being unable to identify the correct corridor. There is no grand metaphor hiding inside the experience of walking past the same general assortment of walls for the fifth time.

Sometimes a building is simply too large, a place is too unfamiliar, and a person has been wandering around for so long that the original task has become an ordeal.

Sometimes the problem is not a lack of purpose.

Sometimes the problem is that you have a perfectly good purpose and absolutely no idea how to accomplish it.

I had direction in the figurative sense.

In the literal sense, I was a disaster.

I kept looking.

I cannot remember every turn I took, every section I passed, or every moment when I convinced myself that the next corridor might finally lead somewhere useful.

What I remember is the feeling of being caught in a place that seemed to have no end.

There were so many directions I could take, but none of them felt reliable. I could continue walking, turn back, look for a landmark, or attempt to find somebody who could point me toward the right place.

Every option seemed obvious in isolation.

Somehow, making a decision remained difficult.

It is embarrassing how quickly an ordinary situation can make you feel helpless when you lose the information you normally rely on.

A familiar environment allows you to move without thinking much about it. You recognise where things are. You remember which direction leads where. You trust your instincts because experience has given you reason to trust them.

Remove that familiarity, and suddenly even a simple walk becomes a series of questions.

Am I going the right way?

Have I passed this place before?

Should I keep going?

Would turning around make things better, or am I about to create an entirely new problem?

I had never expected finding a person in a public establishment to require this much internal negotiation.

By then, I was less interested in exploring anything than I was in finding a recognisable point from which I could make sense of my surroundings.

I wanted one thing to look familiar.

Just one.

I was prepared to develop a personal relationship with a signboard if it meant I could finally stop guessing.

And then there was the matter of my sister.

The longer I spent looking for her, the more absurd it became that two people could occupy the same enormous public place and still be so difficult to reunite.

We were not separated by an ocean. There was no international border involved. I had not lost her in a foreign country where I could not understand a single sign.

We were simply somewhere in the same building.

Somewhere.

That word had begun to lose its usefulness.

Somewhere could mean the floor above me. It could mean the section behind me. It could mean a corridor I had already passed, a corner I had not yet turned, or a place so close to me that the entire situation would become hilarious the moment we finally found each other.

I could only imagine the conversation.

Where were you?

Walking around.

Where?

If I knew, we would not be having this conversation.

I was going to have to explain that I had spent an unreasonable amount of time attempting to locate a person whose existence I was already aware of, in a place where both of us had presumably arrived voluntarily.

It was the kind of story that would sound increasingly stupid the more accurately I described it.

Which, naturally, meant I would probably tell it eventually.

There are few things more reliable than the human desire to turn a miserable experience into a story once the immediate danger has passed.

At the time, however, I was not thinking about how I would narrate the incident later.

I was thinking that I desperately needed to find my sister before this building became my permanent address.

Eventually, the search had to end somehow.

I will spare the record any fabricated account of a dramatic reunion, a miraculous discovery, or a final confrontation with the architect responsible for this architectural abomination.

The details of the search matter less than the fact that, for a while, I genuinely did not know where I was going.

That was the experience.

Not an adventure. Not an expedition. Not a particularly impressive demonstration of independence.

Just a person who had become separated from her sister and discovered that a large public place could feel impossibly complicated when she no longer knew how to navigate it.

I can laugh about it now because the memory has become a story rather than an ongoing problem.

At the time, the situation was considerably less amusing.

There is a difference between knowing that you are probably going to be fine and actually feeling fine while you are lost.

Logic can tell you that there are exits, that people can offer directions, and that a building is unlikely to swallow you whole.

Unfortunately, logic does not always have the authority to dismiss panic the moment it arrives.

Sometimes you have to endure the feeling first and make sense of it afterward.

And sometimes, after all that, you discover that the entire ordeal will eventually become a funny anecdote about how you spent hours wandering around looking for your sister.

Human memory is a strange filing system.

It stores the panic, preserves the embarrassment, and then eventually stamps the whole incident with the classification: FUNNY IN RETROSPECT.

I suppose the lesson here is that large public places should come with a mandatory orientation, a complimentary map, and perhaps a designated adult whose sole responsibility is to prevent me from wandering into another section of the building.

I would also appreciate signs that do more than confirm that I am still inside the establishment.

A sign that says EXIT is useful.

A sign that says YOU ARE HERE is useful, provided that I can determine where HERE is.

A sign that says GOOD LUCK would at least be honest.

I would respect the honesty.

But perhaps I should accept some responsibility for the incident.

I could have approached the problem more methodically. I could have paid closer attention to landmarks, asked for directions sooner, or avoided wandering around without a clear idea of where I was going.

Those would have been reasonable decisions.

Unfortunately, reason and I were apparently not on speaking terms that day.

I was too busy attempting to solve the problem through the revolutionary strategy of walking around and hoping the correct answer would eventually materialise.

It is a strategy I would not recommend.

Particularly if the building is enormous.

Particularly if you have been wandering for hours.

Particularly if your objective is to find your sister and you have somehow managed to make that sound like a military search operation.

I went out to find my sister and nearly developed a personal rivalry with the floor plan.

That is the official summary.

It is also, regrettably, the most accurate one.

I may have gone too far.

Not in the grand, philosophical sense. Not because I have overcomplicated a project or spent several hours redesigning something that was perfectly functional.

This time, I simply wandered too far into a place that was considerably larger than my ability to navigate it.

Somewhere in that enormous establishment, I lost my sense of direction.

Somewhere in that same establishment, my sister was presumably continuing her day without knowing that I had entered into a prolonged disagreement with the building itself.

And somewhere in the middle of all that confusion, I discovered that a person does not need to be in a particularly dangerous situation to feel genuinely overwhelmed.

Sometimes, all it takes is too much space, too many unfamiliar corridors, and no clear idea of which way to go.

I was lost.

I was frustrated.

I was beginning to suspect that the building had more square footage than the entire concept of common sense.

But I kept looking.

Because somewhere in that ridiculous maze of public walkways was my sister, and I was not going to let a building defeat me.

At least, not without making it a very embarrassing story first.

I found myself in an enormous place.

I lost my way.

I spent hours trying to find my sister.

And for a while, I genuinely thought I was fucked.

In retrospect, I probably should have asked for directions.

But that would have made for a considerably shorter article.

End of Entry 002.

Subject eventually ceased wandering. Sister's location: no longer an acceptable mystery. Architectural review: strongly recommended. Sense of direction: presumed missing.
